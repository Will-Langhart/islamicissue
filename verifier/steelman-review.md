> **(1)** The classical Sunni exegetical tradition addresses the apparent tension between the Quran's affirmation of the Torah and Gospel and its polemics against the People of the Book by distinguishing two kinds of tahrif (corruption): tahrif al-lafz (alteration of the actual wording) and tahrif al-ma'na (distortion of meaning or interpretation while the wording remains). Al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir, in their commentaries on verses such as Quran 2:75 ('a party of them used to hear the words of Allah and then distort them knowingly, after they had understood them'), 2:79 ('woe to those who write the scripture with their own hands and then say, this is from Allah'), 4:46, and 5:13, read these as describing real historical instances of scribal and interpretive corruption among some Jewish and Christian communities of the Hijaz. On this reading, the Quran's repeated calls to the People of the Book to 'uphold the Torah and the Gospel' (Quran 5:68) and its instruction to 'ask those who have been reading the Scripture' (Quran 10:94) are calls to return to and honor the authentic core of their revelation, not certifications that every copy in circulation was letter-perfect. The two claims — that the Torah and Gospel are genuine divine revelation, and that corruption (of varying degrees, textual and interpretive) affected communities' handling of them — are held to be compatible rather than contradictory in this framework.
>
> **(2)** A related strand, associated with Ibn Hazm al-Andalusi and later Muslim polemicists working in dialogue with Jewish and Christian sources, locates textual corruption more specifically in the transmission history of the Torah and Gospel — for instance in the multiplicity of variant recensions, the loss of an unbroken chained transmission (isnad) comparable to that developed for hadith, and specific text-critical problems Ibn Hazm catalogues in his Kitab al-Fisal. On this view, a seventh-century reference to 'the Torah' need not presuppose a single, uniformly reliable text; it can refer to a body of material that was substantially available and invokable in argument while also being, in Muslim eyes, imperfectly preserved in certain particulars. This allows the Quran's appeals to function as real, addressable challenges to specific communities holding specific (if imperfect) copies, without requiring a claim of total manuscript perfection.
>
> **(3)** Scholars examining the rhetorical form of verses like Quran 5:43 ('they have the Torah, in which is the judgment of Allah') and 5:47 note that such appeals belong to a common genre of Near Eastern religious polemic: the internal or tu quoque argument, in which an opponent is confronted with the resources of their own tradition ('you possess a scripture that testifies against your present practice; act on it'). This rhetorical strategy does not require the speaker to certify, as a matter of technical textual criticism, that the entirety of the extant document is uncorrupted; it requires only that the material invoked is sufficiently present and recognizable to the audience to make the challenge intelligible. Muslim exegetes such as al-Razi, in discussing the dialectical structure of these Medinan verses, treat them primarily as arguments ad hominem directed at specific Jewish and Christian interlocutors in Muhammad's audience, consistent with the Quran's broader practice of engaging opponents on the terms they themselves acknowledge.

### Proposed strengthened response

> **(1)** Classical Muslim exegesis, in fact, contains two distinct doctrines that later scholarship groups under the umbrella term tahrif, and the more textually cautious of the two — not textual falsification but tahrif al-ma'na, corruption of meaning or interpretation — is arguably the dominant position among the earliest and most authoritative commentators. Al-Tabari (d. 923) in his Jami' al-Bayan, and Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (d. 1210) in his Mafatih al-Ghayb, generally read the Quranic charges of tahrif (2:79; 3:78; 4:46; 5:13, 5:41) as accusing Jewish and Christian scholars of misreading, mistranslating, or willfully misapplying an intact text — 'distorting words from their places' (yuharrifuna al-kalima 'an mawadi'ihi) being read as hermeneutical distortion, concealment of true meaning, and selective citation, not scribal falsification of the manuscripts themselves. Ibn Khaldun's Muqaddimah likewise treats the accusation as one of interpretive infidelity by rabbis and priests rather than manuscript tampering. In the modern period, Muhammad Abduh and his student Rashid Rida, in Tafsir al-Manar, explicitly revived this reading, arguing it is both more consistent with the Quran's own repeated appeals to the Torah and Gospel as extant, consultable texts (Quran 5:47, 10:94) and more defensible given the manuscript evidence available even in their own day. On this view, the seventh-century Torah and Gospel were substantially the texts we possess; the Quran's quarrel is with their misinterpretation and selective application by the religious authorities of the day, not with the integrity of the transmitted text. This position deserves to stand as the primary classical response, with the harder Ibn Hazm-style claim of wholesale scribal falsification (tahrif al-lafz) presented as a later, minority development associated particularly with Andalusian polemics against Judaism and Christianity, formalized by Ibn Hazm (d. 1064) in al-Fisal fi al-Milal.
>
> **(2)** A second and independent line of response concerns the referent of 'al-Injil' itself. Muslim exegetes from al-Tabari onward, and modern apologists such as Ahmed Deedat in Is the Bible God's Word?, have distinguished between al-Injil as the singular revelation given to Jesus — understood analogously to the Torah given to Moses, a body of divine teaching and guidance rather than a book of narrative history — and 'the Gospels' (the four canonical narratives about Jesus compiled by Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John, none of whom the Quran names as recipients of revelation). On this reading, when the Quran commands 'the People of the Gospel' to judge by what God revealed therein (Quran 5:47), it addresses whatever authentic teaching of Jesus survived among them, not an endorsement of the literary form and full content of the canonical Gospels as later assembled. This parallels the well-established distinction between 'Torah' as the Pentateuch and 'Torah' as the broader corpus of Mosaic legal-covenantal revelation, a distinction Jewish tradition itself makes (Torah she-bikhtav vs. Torah she-be'al peh). This reading blunts the critique's assumption that Quranic references to 'the Gospel' straightforwardly denote the four canonical texts in their entirety.
>
> **(3)** On the specific charge that the Quran denies the crucifixion outright (4:157), the classical exegetical tradition is considerably more varied than the critique allows, and this variance is itself a resource for response rather than an embarrassment. The verse's key phrase, wa lakin shubbiha lahum ('but it was made to appear so to them'), generated a substantial literature of interpretation. Substitution theories — that someone else (Judas, Simon of Cyrene, or a volunteer disciple) was crucified in Jesus' place while Jesus was raised bodily to heaven — are found in al-Tabari's Tafsir and became the majority position in later popular exegesis, but they are exegetical elaborations (largely drawing on apocryphal Christian sources such as the Gospel of Basilides as reported by Irenaeus) rather than the plain sense of the Quranic text itself, which asserts only that 'they did not kill him, nor did they crucify him, but it was made to appear so to them' and that 'they have no certain knowledge of it.' Modern scholars such as Mahmoud Ayoub (The Crucifixion in Quranic, Islamic, and Christian Understanding) and Todd Lawson (The Crucifixion and the Quran) have argued the verse is best read not as a bare denial of the historical event of crucifixion but as a denial of Jewish and Roman triumph over Jesus and God's messianic purpose — i.e., a theological claim about vindication (parallel to the Quran's statements that God 'raised him to Himself,' 4:158) rather than a forensic claim about what happened to a body on a cross. On this reading, the verse contests the meaning of the crucifixion (a shameful, God-abandoning death signifying defeat) rather than its occurrence, and Muslim exegesis on this point remains genuinely unsettled rather than monolithically committed to substitution.
>
> **(4)** A further response, advanced by contemporary Muslim apologists such as Shabir Ally and Jamal Badawi, treats the Quran's instruction that Christians 'judge by what God has revealed therein' (5:47) as an internal, dialectical address rather than an epistemic endorsement of the text's perfect transmission. On this reading, the Quran is holding Christians accountable to their own professed standard — arguing, in effect, 'even by your own scripture's teaching you should recognize such-and-such' — a rhetorical strategy attested elsewhere in the Quran's engagement with Jewish and Christian audiences (e.g., 3:93, appealing to the Torah before its revelation was altered by later law). This is analogous to a tu quoque or ad hominem (in the classical, non-pejorative sense of arguing from an opponent's own premises) rather than a certification that the text in Christian hands in the seventh century was textually pristine in the modern text-critical sense. Proponents of this reading note that the Quran elsewhere criticizes specific Jewish and Christian communities for their treatment of scripture in the same rhetorical breath (e.g., 2:75-79), suggesting the address is polemical-corrective rather than epistemically certifying.
>
> **(5)** Finally, some Muslim polemicists dispute the rebuttal's manuscript premise itself, arguing that even granting Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, and the earlier papyri as fourth-century (or earlier) witnesses, this does not establish that the text was uncorrupted in all essentials by the seventh century, since these same manuscript families preserve well-documented internal variants and disputed passages — the Comma Johanneum (1 John 5:7-8), the long ending of Mark (Mark 16:9-20), and the Pericope Adulterae (John 7:53-8:11) among them — which text-critical scholarship itself, using the very apparatus the critique invokes, identifies as later scribal additions absent from the earliest and best manuscripts. Muslim writers making this argument (echoed in some of the exchanges collected at islamicdilemma.net) contend that if the Christian textual tradition itself acknowledges centuries of scribal accretion, harmonization, and emendation prior to and continuing after Muhammad's time, then the claim that 'the seventh-century Bible equals today's Bible in all essentials' concedes too little variance to be dispositive, even if it does not amount to wholesale corruption of the type Ibn Hazm alleged. This argument is best understood as a limited, evidentiary rejoinder — establishing textual fluidity, not proving the specific claims about Christ's deity or atonement were themselves late interpolations — and should be presented with that qualification, since the disputed passages named do not bear directly on the crucifixion or resurrection narratives central to the critique.
>

### Proposed strengthened response

> **(1)** 1. The generic/proper-noun distinction (al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir on Q2:131–132 and Q3:67; cf. Yusuf Ali's commentary). Classical exegetes distinguish between islam as a common noun — the act of submitting (istislam) to God's oneness and command, which is the shared essence of the message of every prophet — and al-Islam as the proper name of the final dispensation revealed through Muhammad and codified in the Shari'a he brought. When the Qur'an calls Abraham, the Egyptian magicians, or the disciples of Jesus 'muslim,' it is applying the verbal/descriptive sense (one who has submitted), exactly as it applies 'kafir' descriptively to anyone who disbelieves, in any age, without implying they belonged to a later-named creedal institution. Ibn Kathir's tafsir on Abraham's du'a in Surat al-Baqara makes this explicit: the milla of Abraham is submission to God's unity, which Muhammad's message restores and completes, not a rival or earlier instance of the same proper-named religion. On this reading there is no contradiction to harmonize in the first place, because 'first Muslim' in Q6:14/6:163 and 'first of the believers' in Q7:143 are not competing claims to have inaugurated the same institution.

2. The philological point on awwal (classical grammarians and mufassirun, e.g., al-Zamakhshari, al-Razi). Arabic awwal, like English 'first,' carries both a temporal sense (prior in sequence) and a rank/excellence sense (foremost, preeminent), and lexicographers and exegetes commonly read Muhammad's 'kuntu awwala al-muslimin' in Q6:14 and 6:163, and Moses' 'ana awwalu l-mu'minin' in Q7:143, as claims to preeminence in submission and faith among their respective peoples — 'the most devoted/foremost in islam,' not 'chronologically first human ever to submit.' This is not a novel apologetic device but a recognized semantic range of the root a-w-l attested elsewhere in the Qur'an and in pre-Islamic and classical Arabic usage. If this reading is accepted, the community-relative harmonization becomes unnecessary as a first line of defense, since no temporal claim is being made that could conflict with Abraham's or the magicians' earlier submission.

3. Din al-fitra and the doctrine of prophetic unity (tafsir tradition broadly, e.g., al-Tabari on Q3:19 'inna al-din 'inda Allah al-islam'; hadith literature on 'al-anbiya' akhwa min 'allat'). ### Proposed strengthened response

> **(1)** The classical Sunni treatment of predestination and freedom is not a single doctrine but three distinct, technically sophisticated positions, each addressing the Qur'anic tension differently. The Ash‘ari school, following Abu al-Hasan al-Ash‘ari and systematized by al-Baqillani and al-Juwayni, teaches kasb ('acquisition'): Allah alone creates (khalq) every act, including human acts, but the human is the locus (mahall) of the act and thereby 'acquires' it, which is sufficient for moral responsibility because acquisition tracks intention (qasd) even though it does not involve independent causal power. This preserves radical divine sovereignty while locating culpability in the will's orientation rather than in efficient causation.
>
> **(2)** The Maturidi school, following Abu Mansur al-Maturidi and refined by later Hanafi theologians such as Abu al-Mu‘in al-Nasafi, sharpens this considerably and should not be collapsed into Ash‘ari kasb. Maturidis hold that the human possesses a real juz' ikhtiyari — a 'partial faculty of choice' — which is itself created by Allah as a standing capacity, but the specific direction the person gives to that capacity (choosing obedience or disobedience) is the human's own, uncreated in the sense of not being a direct divine act of creation in the same manner as the underlying capacity. Allah then creates the actual effect (the act itself, takwin) in response to that human orientation. This is a meaningfully stronger rejoinder to the paradox than kasb, since it locates genuine — if circumscribed — causal agency in the human will itself, rather than merely redescribing divine causation as 'acquired.'
>
> **(3)** The Athari/Hanbali resolution, most fully articulated by Ibn Taymiyya (e.g., in Minhaj al-Sunna and al-Qadar), rejects the kalam apparatus of both kasb and juz' ikhtiyari as unnecessary philosophical mediation and instead affirms both propositions at their plain sense without a mediating mechanism: Allah is the Creator of the human's power, will, and act (in accordance with verses such as 76:30 and 81:29), and the human is nonetheless the real agent (fa‘il) of the act on the level of moral and legal address (khitab). This is presented in the Athari tradition as a middle path deliberately positioned against both the Jabriyya, who deny real human agency, and the Qadariyya/Mu‘tazila, who deny that Allah creates human acts.
>
> **(4)** The Mu‘tazila deserve fuller statement than mere 'affirmation' of libertarian freedom. >
> **(6)** The hadith evidence adduced — the account in Sahih al-Bukhari of the angel writing the fetus's provision, lifespan, and whether it will be of the blessed or wretched, and the tradition concerning the fate of children of polytheists ('Allah knows best what they would have done,' a‘lamu bima kanu ‘amilin) — is addressed directly in the classical commentary tradition, notably al-Nawawi's commentary on Sahih Muslim and Ibn Hajar al-‘Asqalani's Fath al-Bari. >
> **(7)** Finally, al-Ghazali (in Ihya' ‘Ulum al-Din and al-Iqtisad fi al-I‘tiqad) offers a distinct move available across these schools: he treats the metaphysical antinomy between divine creation of acts and human responsibility as a genuine aporia not fully resolvable by discursive theology, but argues that divine justice and the validity of moral address are secured phenomenologically — humans experience real deliberation, weighing, and choice (ikhtiyar), and it is this experienced agency, rather than a settled metaphysical account of ultimate causation, that grounds the legitimacy of praise, blame, and eternal consequence. This position is often invoked as a pastoral and theological stopping-point distinct from, though compatible with, the more technical Ash‘ari, Maturidi, and Athari resolutions above.

This reframes the relationship from 'literary dependence on an apocryphal book' to 'participation in a common regional storytelling milieu,' weakening the charge that the Quran 'copied' a specific canonized-elsewhere text.
>
> **(4)** On the content-level 'purification' claim, a case can be made concretely rather than merely asserted. In the Infancy Gospel of Thomas, the child Jesus's miracles are entangled with petty and at times vindictive episodes (striking a child dead for jostling him, blinding those who complain, quarrels with his teachers) that early Christian authorities themselves rejected as unbefitting a prophet, which is precisely why the text was excluded from the canon. By contrast, in Quran 3:49 and 5:110 the clay-bird miracle is explicitly framed as a sign (aya) performed 'by God's leave' and paired immediately with healing the blind and the leper and raising the dead — miracles drawn from canonical Gospel material — with no trace of the vindictive incidents. Likewise the cradle-speech in Q 19:29–30 is deployed solely to vindicate Mary's chastity and proclaim Jesus's prophethood and servanthood to God, not, as in the Arabic Infancy Gospel, embedded in a chain of wonder-tales performed for display. Muslim commentators (e.g., al-Razi in his discussion of Q 3:49) read this selective retention as evidence that the Quran discriminates between authentic prophetic report and later legendary accretion, retaining only what serves a coherent theological function (establishing Jesus's prophetic status and dependence on God) and discarding what does not.
>
> **(5)** On the Seven Sleepers narrative, the Quran's own text draws attention to sectarian disagreement over the number of sleepers — 'They will say, three, the fourth of them being their dog; and they will say, five, the sixth of them being their dog, guessing at the unseen; and they will say, seven, and the eighth of them being their dog. Say: my Lord knows best their number' (Q 18:22) — and follows this with an explicit caution against disputing over such details except with plain evidence. Tabari and other exegetes read this as the Quran deliberately declining to adjudicate among the variant recensions of the legend circulating among Christian communities of the Byzantine and Syriac world (versions that indeed differed on the number of sleepers, as seen in the Syriac homilies of Jacob of Serugh and the Greek accounts), using the story's moral (God's power over death and resurrection, and reliance on Him amid persecution) while pointedly refusing to endorse any single sectarian version's numerical claims. On this reading the passage is not a naive retelling but an implicit critique of the fixation on legendary minutiae that had come to dominate Christian disputation over the story.
>
> **(6)** On Cain's learning burial from a raven (Q 5:31), Muslim exegetes such as Ibn Kathir and al-Tabari transmit this as part of the isra'iliyyat material received from earlier scriptural communities, explicitly flagged in the classical tradition as reports 'neither to be affirmed nor denied' in their transmitted detail but not doctrinally objectionable, since the underlying moral point (fratricide's horror before God and the origin of burial as a mark of human dignity) is independent of whether the specific raven-motif is historically precise. Comparative work on the motif's appearance in Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer and Targumic tradition is taken by some Muslim scholars as evidence of a common ancient Near Eastern narrative deposit about the first murder — attested divergently across Jewish, and later Quranic, tradition — rather than proof of direct literary dependence on a specific redacted Jewish text whose extant form (Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer is usually dated eighth-century CE, after the Quran) in fact makes the Quran's priority, not dependency, arguable on strict chronological grounds.
>
> **(7)** A final, more synthetic point, following Reynolds's own emphasis on the Quran's 'homiletic' rather than 'narrative' mode: across all four cases, the Quran consistently compresses, moralizes, and reorients these shared stories toward tawhid and prophetic vindication, omitting the narrative elaboration, named authorities, and entertainment value that mark the apocryphal and legendary versions. Muslim scholars argue this consistent redirection — visible differently in each case (signs versus spectacle in the Jesus narratives; refusal of numerical specificity in the Sleepers narrative; moral lesson over romance-adventure in Dhul-Qarnayn; theological point over folkloric detail in the Cain-raven case) — is better explained as purposive theological engagement with a known cultural repertoire than as naive or wholesale absorption of legendary material, and is the concrete cash-value of the 'confirming and correcting' framework rather than a merely asserted general claim.
